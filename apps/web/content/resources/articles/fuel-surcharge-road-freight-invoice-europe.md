# Fuel Surcharge for Road Freight in Europe: How to Calculate It

Diesel is one of the largest and most volatile costs for a transport company. When the price moves and your rate does not, the difference comes out of your margin.

In 2026 that stopped being theoretical. Euronews, citing European Commission data, reported in early October 2026 that average EU diesel prices reached a record of about €2.24 per litre, compared with about €1.59 before the price shock. Hauliers whose contracts had no fuel clause, or a slow one, absorbed much of that increase.

A fuel surcharge solves this, but only if it is agreed clearly, calculated consistently, and shown on the invoice in a way the customer can check. This guide covers all three.

> **Note:** The numbers in this article are illustrative examples, not current fuel prices. Use your own agreed base price, index and consumption figures.

## What a fuel surcharge is

A fuel surcharge is a separate invoice line that adjusts the freight rate when fuel prices move away from an agreed baseline. It is not a fee for a different service. It is a way of keeping the real price of the transport in line with the cost of fuel.

The two most common ways to calculate it:

1. **Percentage method:** a percentage added to the freight rate, based on how far the fuel index has moved from the base price.
2. **Per-kilometre method:** an amount per kilometre, based on the fuel price difference and an agreed consumption figure.

A third, simpler option is a **price-band table**, where each range of diesel prices maps to a fixed percentage.

## Step 1: Agree it before the load

A surcharge you invented after the delivery is a dispute waiting to happen. Put these points in the rate agreement or transport order:

- Which fuel index is used, and which country or region it covers
- The **base price** (the price at which the surcharge is zero)
- How often it is adjusted (weekly or monthly)
- **Which week's price applies**, for example the Monday price of the week before loading
- The **fuel share** of the rate, if you use the percentage method
- The consumption figure, if you use the per-kilometre method
- Whether the surcharge can go **negative** when the price falls below the base
- How the result is rounded

## Step 2: Choose a neutral index

Use a published source that both sides can check. One widely used public source is the European Commission's **Weekly Oil Bulletin**, which presents weekly consumer prices for petroleum products in all EU countries. It publishes Monday prices a few days later, so decide in the agreement which bulletin date applies to which load.

Points to settle:

- **Country or EU average.** Use the country where most of the fuel is bought, or the EU average, and write it down.
- **With or without taxes.** The same basis must be used for the base price and the current price. Mixing a with-tax base and a without-tax current price gives a wrong surcharge. Check which series you are reading.
- **Currency.** For countries outside the euro area, the Commission converts prices to euro at the exchange rate of that week.

You can use another index, such as a national fuel index or your fuel-card supplier's published price list, as long as both parties accept it in writing.

## Step 3: Calculate the surcharge

### Percentage method

```
Surcharge % = ((Current price − Base price) ÷ Base price) × Fuel share of the rate
Surcharge   = Freight rate × Surcharge %
```

**Example (illustrative):**

| Item | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Base diesel price | €1.60 / litre |
| Current diesel price | €1.90 / litre |
| Fuel share of the rate | 30% |
| Freight rate | €1,850.00 |

- Price change: (1.90 − 1.60) ÷ 1.60 = 18.75%
- Surcharge %: 18.75% × 30% = 5.625%
- Surcharge: €1,850.00 × 5.625% = **€104.06**
- Total: €1,954.06

**Rounding matters.** If you round the percentage to 5.63% first, the surcharge becomes €104.16. A ten-cent difference is small, but unclear rounding rules cause arguments. Agree one rule and apply it every time.

**If the price falls:** with a current price of €1.50, the result is (1.50 − 1.60) ÷ 1.60 × 30% = −1.875%, which is **−€34.69**. Decide in advance whether the surcharge can become a credit or is floored at zero.

### Per-kilometre method

```
Surcharge per km = (Current price − Base price) × Consumption (litres per km)
Surcharge        = Surcharge per km × Distance
```

**Example (illustrative):** price difference €0.30 per litre, agreed consumption 32 litres per 100 km (0.32 litres per km), distance 900 km.

- €0.30 × 0.32 = €0.096 per km
- €0.096 × 900 km = **€86.40**

Use an agreed standard consumption figure, not the real consumption of one truck on one trip.

### Price-band table

| Diesel price (€/litre) | Surcharge on freight rate |
| --- | --- |
| up to 1.60 | 0% |
| 1.61 – 1.80 | 3% |
| 1.81 – 2.00 | 6% |
| 2.01 – 2.20 | 9% |

These bands are an example only. Bands are simple to apply but move in steps, so check that the steps are small enough to reflect real changes.

## Step 4: Show it on the invoice

A single line reading "Fuel surcharge €104.06" invites questions. Show how it was calculated, so the customer can check it without calling you.

Copy-paste description (replace the placeholders):

```
Fuel surcharge — [index name], diesel, [country], week of [date]:
€[current]/l vs base €[base]/l, fuel share [x]% — [y]% of €[freight rate]
```

Practical rules:

- Keep the surcharge on its **own line**, separate from the freight rate.
- Put the **load or order reference** on the line or in the invoice notes.
- Apply the surcharge to the freight rate only, unless the agreement says it also applies to other charges.
- Use the same index, base price and rounding rule on every invoice for the same customer.

## VAT on surcharges

A fuel surcharge is normally part of the price charged for the transport service. For VAT it is therefore usually treated in the same way as the freight charge itself. Because VAT depends on the customer, the countries involved and the exact transaction, confirm the treatment with your accountant.

## Check the national rules

In some countries, fuel-price adjustment in road freight contracts is regulated, not just a matter of contract. A 2026 benchmark by IRU, Upply and Transport Intelligence noted that Spain, France and, to some extent, Italy have national fuel-indexation systems, and Spain tightened its mandatory fuel-price revision mechanism for road freight contracts in April 2026 through Royal Decree-law 9/2026.

If your contract falls under one of these systems, the official formula, thresholds and invoicing requirements may override what you would otherwise agree. Check the rules for the country whose law governs your contract.

## Common mistakes

- **No written agreement** on the index, base price and timing
- **Mixing price bases**, such as a with-tax base and a without-tax current price
- **Changing the index** mid-contract without telling the customer
- **Applying the surcharge on top of other extras**, not just the freight rate
- **Unclear rounding**, leading to small mismatches between invoices
- **Ignoring price falls**, which damages trust when prices rise again
- **Adding a surcharge afterwards** to an agreement that did not include one

## Checklist before you issue the invoice

- Is the surcharge covered by the agreement or transport order?
- Did you use the agreed index and the agreed week?
- Are base price and current price on the same basis (with or without taxes)?
- Is the calculation shown on the invoice line?
- Is the load or order reference included?
- Did you apply the agreed rounding rule?
- Does any national fuel-indexation rule apply to this contract?

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a fuel surcharge mandatory?

In most cases it is a matter of the contract. Some countries regulate fuel-price adjustment, so check the rules that apply to your contract.

### Which fuel index should I use?

The one both sides have agreed in writing. A published, public source such as the Commission's Weekly Oil Bulletin is easy for both parties to verify.

### Can I add a surcharge if the contract does not mention one?

Do not assume so. If the agreement is silent, you may need the customer's agreement before applying one. Agree it before the load, not after the invoice.

### Should the surcharge be on the same invoice as the freight?

Usually yes, as a separate line with its calculation shown.

### What if the fuel price drops?

That depends on what you agreed. If the clause is symmetrical, the surcharge can become a credit. If not, it can be floored at zero. Write the rule down.
