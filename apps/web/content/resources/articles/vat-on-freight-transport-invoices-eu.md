# VAT on Freight Transport Invoices in the EU: B2B, Reverse Charge and Common Exceptions

VAT on a freight transport invoice can look simple until the carrier and customer are established in different countries.

For transport companies operating across the European Union, the first question is not simply:

**“What VAT rate should I put on the invoice?”**

The more useful questions are:

1. Is the customer a business or a private consumer?
2. Where is the customer established for VAT purposes?
3. Is the transport service covered by the general B2B place-of-supply rule?
4. Who is liable to account for the VAT?
5. Does a specific exception change the result?
6. What wording must appear on the invoice?

For many cross-border B2B freight services, the general EU rule places the supply where the business customer is established. When the customer is liable for the VAT under the reverse-charge mechanism, the supplier generally does not charge its own local VAT and the customer accounts for the tax.

That is the general framework — not a substitute for checking the exact transaction.

> **Important:** VAT treatment depends on the facts of the transaction and the applicable national rules. This article is a practical overview, not tax or legal advice. Have your accountant or VAT adviser confirm the treatment you use.

## The general B2B rule for freight transport services

Under the EU VAT Directive's general rule for services supplied business-to-business, the place of taxation is generally where the business customer is established.

In simplified form:

**EU carrier → business customer in another EU country → customer country is generally the place of supply**

For example:

- Carrier established in Germany
- Business customer established in the Netherlands
- Freight transport service supplied to that Dutch business

Assuming the general B2B rule applies and no relevant exception changes the treatment, the service is generally taxed where the Dutch customer is established.

The practical invoice result is commonly:

- Supplier does not charge German VAT
- Customer accounts for VAT in the Netherlands under reverse charge
- Invoice contains the required reverse-charge wording

The European Commission describes the general B2B services rule under Article 44 of the VAT Directive: the place of taxation is where the customer is established.

## What does reverse charge mean?

Normally, the supplier charges VAT and later reports it to the tax authority.

Under reverse charge, liability for the VAT moves to the customer.

The supplier issues the invoice according to the applicable reverse-charge treatment, while the customer accounts for the VAT in its own VAT return.

Under Article 196 of the VAT Directive, the customer is liable for VAT for certain services where the place of supply follows the Article 44 customer-location rule and the supplier is not established in the Member State where the VAT is due.

This mechanism is common in cross-border B2B services within the EU.

## What should the invoice say when reverse charge applies?

EU VAT invoicing rules require the invoice to include the words:

**“reverse charge”**

when the customer is liable for the VAT.

Some businesses also include a more descriptive legal note, for example:

> Reverse charge — VAT payable by the customer under Article 196 of Council Directive 2006/112/EC.

The exact wording and any additional national requirements should be checked for the countries involved.

The key point is that an invoice should not silently show zero VAT without explaining why the customer is responsible for the tax.

## Example: cross-border B2B road freight service

Suppose:

- Carrier: Austria
- Customer: Germany
- Transport service: Austria to Germany
- Customer is a VAT-registered business acting as a business customer
- No special exception changes the place-of-supply treatment

A simplified invoice could contain:

| Field | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Supplier | Austrian Transport GmbH |
| Customer | German Logistics GmbH |
| Net transport service | €2,000 |
| Supplier VAT charged | €0 |
| VAT treatment | Reverse charge |
| Legal text | Reverse charge — VAT payable by the customer |

The customer then handles the VAT according to the rules applicable in its country.

Do not copy this example blindly. The actual invoice must reflect the real transaction.

## VAT IDs matter

For cross-border B2B invoicing, the customer's VAT status is important.

Businesses should normally verify the customer's VAT identification information and retain the relevant business records.

The European Commission's VAT rules distinguish between services supplied to a taxable person acting as such and services supplied to a private consumer.

That distinction can change the place-of-supply rule entirely.

## B2B and B2C are not the same

The general B2B services rule is not the general B2C rule.

For B2B services:

**Place of taxation → generally where the customer is established**

For ordinary B2C services:

**Place of taxation → generally where the supplier is established**

However, freight transport has additional B2C rules that can matter.

For intra-Community transport of goods supplied to a private consumer, the place of supply is generally tied to where the transport begins.

That means a carrier should not apply a B2B reverse-charge workflow to a consumer transaction merely because the transport crosses an EU border.

## Transport connected to a sale of goods is different from a separately supplied freight service

Another common source of confusion is mixing:

- A freight transport service supplied by a carrier, and
- Transport costs that form part of a supplier's sale of goods.

If a seller charges its customer for goods and transport as part of the same commercial supply, the VAT treatment may follow the treatment of that supply of goods rather than being treated as a separate carrier-to-customer freight service.

This distinction matters when deciding whether a transport charge should be treated independently.

## Domestic freight transport

If both the relevant supplier and transaction are domestic, the cross-border reverse-charge example above does not automatically apply.

Domestic VAT treatment depends on:

- The Member State
- The type of customer
- The supplier's VAT position
- Applicable national reverse-charge rules, if any
- The exact transaction

EU rules provide a baseline, but Member States retain national invoicing rules in certain areas.

Never assume that a domestic invoice should be handled the same way as a cross-border B2B freight invoice.

## Freight involving a country outside the EU

Transport involving the UK, Switzerland, Norway, Serbia, Turkey, North Macedonia, or another non-EU country requires separate analysis.

“Europe” is not one VAT jurisdiction.

The EU VAT Directive applies to EU Member States, while non-EU countries have their own VAT or indirect-tax systems.

For a route such as:

**Germany → Switzerland**

or:

**Serbia → Austria**

the invoice treatment can involve different rules from a purely intra-EU B2B service.

The origin, destination, contractual customer, customs context, and local tax rules can all matter.

## What information should a reverse-charge freight invoice contain?

Besides the ordinary invoice details required under applicable VAT rules, a cross-border B2B freight invoice may need to include:

- Unique invoice number
- Invoice date
- Supplier legal name and address
- Customer legal name and address
- Supplier VAT identification number
- Customer VAT identification number where required
- Description of the freight transport service
- Service/supply date where required
- Net amount
- VAT treatment
- The words **“reverse charge”** where the customer is liable for VAT
- Payment terms
- Currency
- Other nationally required details

For the wider EU invoice baseline, see:

## What about the CMR consignment note?

A CMR consignment note and a freight invoice serve different purposes.

The CMR consignment note documents the contract of carriage and transport information for international road freight where the CMR Convention applies.

The invoice requests payment for the service.

A transport company may therefore retain both:

**CMR / transport document → evidence and shipment information**

**Invoice → commercial and VAT billing document**

Do not treat the CMR note as a replacement for a VAT invoice.

## Common VAT mistakes on freight invoices

### Charging the supplier's local VAT automatically

A cross-border B2B service may instead fall under the customer-location and reverse-charge rules.

### Using reverse charge for every international load

International transport does not automatically mean reverse charge.

You still need to determine:

- Customer status
- Place of supply
- Supplier/customer establishment
- Applicable exception
- National rules

### Writing “0% VAT” without explaining the treatment

Zero-rated, exempt, outside scope, and reverse charge are not interchangeable concepts.

If reverse charge applies, the EU invoicing rules require the relevant reverse-charge wording.

### Not checking the customer's VAT status

B2B and B2C place-of-supply rules differ.

### Treating every transport-related charge as a standalone freight service

Transport included in a sale of goods can have a different VAT analysis.

### Treating all European countries as EU VAT jurisdictions

The UK, Switzerland, Norway, Serbia, Turkey, North Macedonia and other European countries are not governed by the EU VAT Directive in the same way as EU Member States.

## What should a small transport company verify before issuing the invoice?

Before finalising a cross-border freight invoice, confirm:

- Who is the contractual customer?
- Is the customer acting as a taxable business?
- Where is that customer established?
- Is its VAT identification information valid and relevant to the transaction?
- Where is the supplier established?
- Does the general B2B services rule apply?
- Is the customer liable for the VAT?
- Does the invoice need the words “reverse charge”?
- Does a national or transaction-specific exception apply?
- Is the transport being invoiced as a separate service or as part of another supply?

If the answer to one of those questions is unclear, resolve it before issuing the invoice.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does an EU carrier always charge VAT on a freight invoice?

No. The correct treatment depends on the transaction. For many cross-border B2B services, the general place-of-supply rule places taxation where the business customer is established, and reverse charge may apply.

### Does reverse charge mean the service is VAT-free?

No. Reverse charge changes who is responsible for accounting for VAT. It is not the same thing as saying VAT does not exist.

### What wording should appear on a reverse-charge invoice?

EU VAT invoicing rules require the words **“reverse charge”** when the customer is liable for the tax. Additional wording may be used depending on the transaction and national practice.

### Can I use reverse charge for a private customer?

Do not assume so. B2C services follow different place-of-supply rules, and intra-Community B2C transport of goods has specific rules.

### Is a CMR consignment note the same as an invoice?

No. A CMR note is a transport document connected with the contract of carriage. An invoice is the commercial/tax billing document.

### Does InvoiceTrucker automatically determine whether reverse charge applies?

No. VAT treatment must be determined by the business based on the transaction and applicable rules.

## The safest approach: decide the VAT treatment before invoicing

For EU freight businesses, invoice accuracy starts before the invoice is generated.

The sequence should be:

**Customer → VAT status → Place of supply → VAT liability → Invoice wording → Supporting transport documents**

That avoids the common mistake of first creating an invoice and only later trying to decide why VAT was or was not charged.
