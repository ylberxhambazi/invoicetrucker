# How to Create a Trucking Invoice: A Step-by-Step Guide

Creating a trucking invoice starts before you type an invoice number.

A reliable billing process connects the **rate confirmation, load details, delivery paperwork, charges, invoice, and payment status** so that everything matches before the invoice reaches the customer.

A simple workflow looks like this:

**Rate Confirmation → Completed Load → POD/BOL → Invoice → Send → Track → Paid**

For a small fleet, the goal is not to make invoicing complicated. It is to make sure every completed load becomes a correct invoice and every invoice remains visible until it is paid.

## How to create a trucking invoice in 8 steps

1. Verify the load and customer information.
2. Review the agreed rate.
3. Collect the POD and supporting paperwork.
4. Create a unique invoice number.
5. Add line-haul and additional charges.
6. Review the invoice against the load documents.
7. Send the complete billing package.
8. Track the invoice until payment.

Let's go through each step.

## 1. Verify the load information

Start with the load itself.

Before creating the invoice, confirm:

* Customer or broker name
* Load number
* Pickup location
* Delivery location
* Pickup date
* Delivery date
* Driver
* Truck or unit number, if relevant

The load number is especially important because it usually connects your invoice to the broker or customer's internal system.

If the invoice contains the wrong load reference, even a correct amount may require manual investigation before payment.

For a growing fleet, load information should ideally be entered once and reused rather than copied manually into multiple spreadsheets and documents.

## 2. Check the rate confirmation

The rate confirmation should be one of the main references used to prepare the invoice.

Check:

* Agreed line-haul amount
* Fuel surcharge
* Pickup and delivery details
* Additional stops
* Special instructions
* Payment terms
* Approved accessorials

Your invoice should agree with the commercial terms already documented.

For example:

| Charge         | Rate Confirmation | Invoice |
| -------------- | ----------------: | ------: |
| Line haul      |            $2,450 |  $2,450 |
| Fuel surcharge |              $325 |    $325 |
| Total          |            $2,775 |  $2,775 |

If the two documents do not match, resolve the difference before sending the invoice.

Invoice and document mismatches are a common source of billing rework and payment delays.

## 3. Collect the delivery paperwork

Do not create the invoice and then start searching for the documents.

Collect the required paperwork first.

Depending on the customer or broker, this may include:

* Signed Proof of Delivery
* Bill of Lading
* Rate confirmation
* Lumper receipts
* Scale tickets
* Detention approval
* Revised rate confirmation
* Additional stop approval

Bills of lading and similar shipping documents are also part of the supporting records commonly used throughout trucking operations. FMCSA identifies bills of lading, trip records, expense receipts, electronic communications, and settlement records among normal supporting-document categories.

The exact billing package varies by customer, so know the requirements before sending the invoice.

## 4. Create a unique invoice number

Every invoice needs its own identifier.

A simple sequential format is enough:

* INV-1001
* INV-1002
* INV-1003

You can also include the year:

* 2026-1001
* 2026-1002

Avoid changing numbering systems constantly.

A consistent invoice number helps you:

* Find invoices quickly
* Track payments
* Avoid duplicates
* Reconcile records
* Communicate with customers
* Identify missing invoices

The invoice number should never depend entirely on the load number because one system belongs to your business and the other may belong to the broker or customer.

## 5. Add the carrier and customer information

The invoice should clearly identify both parties.

### Carrier information

Include:

* Legal business name
* Business address
* Phone
* Email
* MC number when applicable
* USDOT number when applicable

### Customer information

Include:

* Customer or broker name
* Billing address
* Billing contact
* Billing email
* Account/reference information if required

Keep this information consistent across invoices.

Typing customer details manually every time creates unnecessary opportunities for mistakes.

## 6. Add the charges

Now add the money.

Start with the base line-haul charge.

For example:

| Description    |        Amount |
| -------------- | ------------: |
| Line haul      |     $2,450.00 |
| Fuel surcharge |       $325.00 |
| **Subtotal**   | **$2,775.00** |

Then add any legitimate additional charges.

These may include:

* Detention
* Lumper fees
* Layover
* Stop-off
* Driver assist
* Reconsignment
* Truck ordered not used

Example:

| Description    |        Amount |
| -------------- | ------------: |
| Line haul      |     $2,450.00 |
| Fuel surcharge |       $325.00 |
| Detention      |       $150.00 |
| Lumper fee     |        $85.00 |
| **Total Due**  | **$3,010.00** |

Do not combine every charge into one unexplained amount.

A customer should be able to compare the invoice against the rate confirmation and supporting documentation quickly.

## 7. Verify accessorial charges

Accessorials deserve extra attention because they are a common source of disputes.

Suppose your original rate confirmation shows:

**$2,775**

but your final invoice shows:

**$3,010**

That can be completely valid.

But you need to explain the difference.

For example:

* $150 detention
* $85 lumper fee

Then make sure the corresponding documentation exists.

If the detention required written approval, attach it.

If you paid a lumper, attach the receipt.

If the broker issued a revised rate confirmation, keep that document connected to the load.

A strong invoice does not simply ask for more money. It makes the reason for every additional charge obvious.

## 8. Add payment terms and remittance details

Tell the customer when and where to pay.

Payment information may include:

* Net 15
* Net 30
* Net 45
* QuickPay terms
* Remittance address
* ACH instructions
* Factoring company instructions
* Payment portal information

Payment terms should match the agreement you already have with the customer or broker.

If you use factoring, follow the factor's remittance instructions carefully.

An invoice can be approved correctly and still become delayed if payment instructions are unclear or inconsistent.

## 9. Review the invoice before sending it

This is one of the most important steps.

Compare the invoice against the other documents.

Check:

### Invoice vs rate confirmation

Does the line-haul amount match?

Does the fuel surcharge match?

Are additional charges approved?

### Invoice vs POD/BOL

Does the load number match?

Are pickup and delivery details correct?

Is the delivery paperwork signed when required?

### Invoice vs customer information

Is the billing company correct?

Is the invoice being sent to the correct email?

Are payment instructions current?

A one-minute review before sending the invoice can prevent days of back-and-forth later.

## 10. Send the complete billing package

Do not think of the invoice as an isolated PDF.

Think of the billing package.

For example:

**Invoice**

*

**Signed POD**

*

**Rate Confirmation**

*

**Accessorial Documentation**

The customer's requirements determine exactly what needs to be included.

A clean billing package makes the accounts-payable team's job easier.

That matters because easier invoices are usually easier to approve.

## 11. Track the invoice after sending it

Sending the invoice is not the final step.

The invoice should have a status.

A simple workflow might use:

* Draft
* Ready to send
* Sent
* Due
* Overdue
* Paid

Small fleets often lose visibility here.

The invoice exists.

The email was sent.

Then nobody looks at it again until cash flow feels tight.

At that point someone opens Excel and starts asking:

**Which invoices are still unpaid?**

Your invoicing process should answer that question immediately.

## Example trucking invoice workflow

Imagine a carrier completes this load:

**Broker:** Example Logistics
**Load:** 48291
**Route:** Chicago, IL → Nashville, TN
**Line haul:** $2,400
**Fuel surcharge:** $300
**Lumper:** $75
**Delivery:** September 14, 2026

After delivery:

1. Driver sends the signed POD.
2. Lumper receipt is uploaded.
3. Dispatcher verifies the load details.
4. Invoice INV-1048 is generated.
5. Charges are entered.

| Charge         |     Amount |
| -------------- | ---------: |
| Line haul      |     $2,400 |
| Fuel surcharge |       $300 |
| Lumper         |        $75 |
| **Total**      | **$2,775** |

6. The invoice is checked against the rate confirmation.
7. POD and lumper receipt are attached.
8. Billing package is emailed to the broker.
9. Invoice status changes to **Sent**.
10. Payment due date is tracked.
11. When the payment arrives, status changes to **Paid**.

That is a complete invoicing workflow.

## What should you avoid when creating trucking invoices?

### Creating invoices from memory

Use the actual load documents.

Do not rely on someone remembering what the agreed rate was.

### Copying an old invoice

Using an old invoice as a template can leave behind:

* Wrong customer
* Wrong load number
* Wrong dates
* Wrong charges
* Wrong payment instructions

Templates are useful.

Duplicating previous transactions is risky.

### Sending before collecting paperwork

An invoice without required POD or supporting documentation may simply come back.

### Combining loads

Unless the customer's billing process specifically allows it, avoid making an invoice difficult to reconcile by mixing unrelated loads.

### Ignoring rejected invoices

A rejected invoice is not a completed invoice.

Fix the issue, resend it, and continue tracking it.

### Tracking invoices only by email

An email inbox is not an accounts-receivable system.

You need to know which invoices are:

**sent, due, overdue, and paid.**

## Should I create invoices immediately after delivery?

Usually, faster is better once the load is complete and all required documentation is available.

If a load is delivered Monday and you wait until Friday to invoice it, four days have already passed before the customer's payment process even begins.

A good workflow aims to move from:

**Delivered → Documentation Complete → Invoice Sent**

with as little unnecessary delay as possible.

## What is the difference between a rate confirmation and an invoice?

A rate confirmation documents the agreed terms of the load between the parties.

An invoice requests payment after the service has been performed.

The rate confirmation helps determine what should appear on the invoice, but they are different documents.

## What is the difference between a BOL and an invoice?

A Bill of Lading is a shipping document connected to the transportation of freight.

An invoice is a request for payment.

The shipment paperwork supports the billing process, but a BOL does not replace a properly created invoice in most normal carrier billing workflows.

## Do I need a POD to create an invoice?

You can technically prepare an invoice before you have the POD, but many customers and brokers will not process payment without required delivery documentation.

For that reason, a practical workflow is:

**Delivery → signed POD → invoice → billing package.**

## Can I create trucking invoices in Excel?

Yes.

Excel can work well for a small operation.

The difficulty usually starts when you need to manage:

* Many loads
* Multiple customers
* Supporting documents
* Invoice statuses
* Due dates
* Accessorials
* Outstanding balances
* Multiple employees

At that point, creating the invoice is not the main problem anymore.

Keeping the entire process organized is.

## What should happen after the invoice is paid?

Mark the invoice as paid and retain the relevant records according to your company's accounting, tax, contractual, and regulatory requirements.

Your system should preserve a clear history of:

* Invoice number
* Customer
* Load
* Invoice date
* Due date
* Amount
* Payment date
* Supporting documents

This becomes increasingly important as invoice volume grows.

## Build an invoice process, not just an invoice

A good trucking invoice is only one piece of a good billing workflow.

The real process is:

**Load → Documents → Invoice → Sent → Due → Paid**

When each step is visible, it becomes much harder for completed loads or unpaid invoices to disappear inside spreadsheets and inboxes.
