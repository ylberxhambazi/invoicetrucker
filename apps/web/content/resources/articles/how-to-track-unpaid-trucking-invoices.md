# How to Track Unpaid Trucking Invoices and Follow Up on Late Payments

Creating an invoice is not the same as getting paid.

For owner-operators and small fleets, one of the easiest ways to create cash-flow problems is to treat invoicing as a finished task once the PDF has been emailed.

The real workflow is:

**Completed Load → Invoice → Sent → Due → Paid**

Anything that gets stuck between **Sent** and **Paid** needs attention.

A simple invoice-tracking system can help you answer:

- Who owes us money?
- How much is due?
- Which invoice is overdue?
- Which customer needs follow-up?
- Which invoice is missing paperwork?
- Which payment has arrived but not been recorded?

## Why unpaid invoice tracking matters

Trucking has constant operating costs: fuel, insurance, tires, repairs, tolls, payroll, factoring fees, truck payments, and permits.

If invoices are not tracked, the business can look profitable on paper while cash remains tied up in receivables.

The problem is often not lack of revenue. It is lack of visibility.

## Start with clear invoice statuses

Every invoice should have a status.

A simple system is:

- Draft
- Ready to send
- Sent
- Due
- Overdue
- Paid
- Disputed

You may use different labels, but the important part is that each invoice is in one clear state.

## Track the invoice date and due date

Do not track only the invoice date. You also need:

- Invoice date
- Sent date
- Payment term
- Due date
- Paid date

Example:

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Invoice | INV-1048 |
| Sent | October 3 |
| Terms | Net 30 |
| Due | November 2 |
| Paid | — |

This invoice is not overdue on October 20. It becomes overdue only after the agreed due date passes.

## Use invoice aging

Invoice aging groups receivables by how late they are.

Typical buckets:

- Current
- 1–30 days overdue
- 31–60 days overdue
- 61–90 days overdue
- 90+ days overdue

| Customer | Invoice | Balance | Aging |
| --- | --- | ---: | --- |
| ABC Logistics | INV-1041 | $2,800 | Current |
| XYZ Freight | INV-1032 | $1,950 | 8 days overdue |
| Roadway Broker | INV-1008 | $3,400 | 37 days overdue |

This immediately tells you where to focus.

## Check whether the invoice was actually accepted

Before following up on payment, make sure the invoice entered the customer’s billing process.

Ask:

- Was it sent to the correct email?
- Was it uploaded to the correct portal?
- Did the broker require POD/BOL?
- Was the rate confirmation attached?
- Were accessorial receipts included?
- Did the customer reject anything?

A payment delay may really be a document problem.

## Follow up before an invoice becomes seriously overdue

A simple follow-up rhythm might be:

### Before due date

No aggressive follow-up. Check only for document or approval problems.

### On the due date

Confirm that payment is scheduled.

### 3–5 days overdue

Send a polite payment-status request.

### 7–14 days overdue

Follow up again and ask whether anything is blocking payment.

### 30+ days overdue

Escalate according to your contract, customer relationship, and internal process.

The exact timing should reflect your customer agreements.

## Keep follow-up factual

Avoid emotional or vague messages.

A good payment follow-up contains:

- Invoice number
- Load number
- Invoice date
- Due date
- Balance
- Original invoice attachment or link
- Request for payment status

For example:

> Hello, we are following up on invoice INV-1048 for load 48291. The invoice balance is $2,800 and the due date was November 2. Please confirm the current payment status and let us know if any supporting documentation is missing.

Short and clear.

## Track disputes separately

Not every unpaid invoice is simply late. Some are disputed.

Common reasons include:

- Rate mismatch
- Missing POD
- Accessorial disagreement
- Damage claim
- Duplicate invoice
- Wrong customer/load reference
- Incorrect payment terms

Mark disputed invoices separately so they do not disappear inside a generic overdue list.

## Track partial payments

Sometimes the customer pays part of the invoice.

Invoice total: **$3,000**  
Payment received: **$2,700**  
Outstanding: **$300**

Do not mark the invoice simply as paid. Track the total, payments received, and remaining balance.

## Track payment references

When payment arrives, record useful references such as:

- ACH reference
- Check number
- Payment date
- Amount
- Remittance reference

This helps when multiple invoices are paid together.

## Customer payment behavior matters

Over time, invoice data can show patterns.

You may discover:

- Broker A normally pays in 21 days
- Broker B says Net 30 but pays in 40
- Customer C often requests missing paperwork
- Broker D frequently disputes accessorials

This is operational intelligence. A customer’s freight rate is only part of the relationship. Payment reliability matters too.

## What should an owner-operator track in a spreadsheet?

If you still use Excel or Google Sheets, keep at least:

- Invoice number
- Customer
- Load number
- Invoice date
- Sent date
- Due date
- Amount
- Amount paid
- Balance
- Status
- Last follow-up date
- Notes

That is enough to create a basic accounts-receivable view.

The problem comes when this information is split across spreadsheets, email, PDFs, driver messages, bank accounts, and broker portals.

## A simple weekly receivables routine

Once per week:

1. Review all open invoices.
2. Check invoices due in the next 7 days.
3. Identify anything overdue.
4. Confirm missing paperwork.
5. Send required follow-ups.
6. Record payments received.
7. Update disputes.
8. Review total outstanding balance.

This can take far less time than chasing invoices randomly throughout the week.

## What metrics should a small fleet watch?

Useful basic metrics include:

### Total outstanding

How much money customers currently owe.

### Overdue total

How much is past due.

### Oldest unpaid invoice

The invoice that has been waiting longest.

### Average days to payment

How quickly customers actually pay.

### Percentage overdue

How much of receivables is late.

You do not need an enterprise finance department to use these numbers.

## Common unpaid-invoice mistakes

### No due date

You cannot tell what is late.

### No status

Every invoice simply looks “open.”

### No follow-up history

Nobody knows whether the customer was contacted.

### Mixing drafts with receivables

Draft invoices are not money owed yet.

### Forgetting partial payments

The balance becomes inaccurate.

### Chasing the wrong problem

The customer may be waiting for a missing POD, not refusing payment.

## When should you stop hauling for a slow-paying customer?

That is a business decision, but repeated late payment should be part of the decision.

Consider the amount outstanding, age of invoices, customer communication, dispute history, contract terms, future load profitability, and overall relationship.

Do not evaluate a load only by the rate if collection is consistently difficult.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should I check unpaid invoices?

At least weekly for a small operation. Higher-volume fleets may need daily review.

### When is an invoice overdue?

When the agreed due date has passed and payment has not been received.

### Should I contact the broker on the due date?

A polite payment-status check is reasonable, especially if payment timing matters to your cash flow.

### What if the customer says paperwork is missing?

Send the required documents promptly and record the reason for the delay.

### Should paid invoices stay in the system?

Yes. Paid invoices are part of your billing history and help with reporting and reconciliation.

That visibility makes cash flow easier to manage.
